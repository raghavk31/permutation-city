Permutation City · a sandbox for designing with nature

What could be here, if the land wrote the rules?

Pick a site. Its ground, water, flooding, green and existing city are read from open data and stacked, as Ian McHarg did in Design with Nature. The stack becomes rules for what can be built where. Inside those rules, designs are written as genes (attractors that pull density, green and water) and bred across generations, scored on flood storage, heat, embodied carbon, daylight and human scale. You play the urban designer: lock what you like, breed by hand, and throw a storm at it.

01 · what is here

The layers

Each cell is one unit of design: a 20 m hex (centre to corner), the unit of the studio this grew from. Hover for every layer at once.

02 · what the land allows

The rules

Stacked, the layers decide each cell: forbidden (water, deep flooding, the water's edge), retained (the existing city outside the design area), constrained (buildable, with height caps, permeable ground and allowed uses), or open. Evolution can explore inside these rules but never break them.

03 · one design

A genome

A design is 47 numbers. Eight attractors, each a position, a reach, a strength and a kind (circles: solid pulls building density, dashed pulls green, dotted pulls water), and seven site-wide genes. Water claims its share first, then green; the rest is building, rising towards the cores.

04 · generations

Evolution

Designs within 10% of the floor-area brief always beat those outside it. Among them, a design survives if no other is better on every score at once. Parents swap whole attractors, 60/40; a few genes mutate; a tenth of each generation are newcomers; near-copies lose their place, so distinct families survive.

That is NSGA-II, the default. NSGA-III keeps the same breeding but spreads survivors over fixed directions through the five scores instead of keeping the most distinct. MAP-Elites asks a different question: not the best trade-offs, but the best design of every kind. Pick two traits; it keeps the best design in each cell of a grid over them (tall and open, low and dense, and so on), even where those designs lose on the scores.

Any form metric with a better end (chapter 05 lists them) can join the five scores as an objective, up to two. With more than five objectives crowding stops telling designs apart, so "auto" switches to NSGA-III.

05 · the trade-offs

Explore

Every design the run kept. Blue designs are on the front (on the brief, and no other kept design beats them on every score); grey ones are beaten; hollow ones miss the floor-area brief. Each axis runs from worse to better. Point at a design to find it, click to look at it.

Beside the scores, each design has thirty form metrics: human scale, permeability, nature, harmony and variety, and Alexander's properties of wholeness (levels of scale, strong centres, boundaries, rhythm, positive space, symmetries, contrast, gradients, roughness, the void). The streets and blocks they need are read off each design: blocks are joined building cells, cut where an existing road crosses. They are readouts for judging, not a verdict.

07 · by hand

Breeding

Pick two parents and breed six children; keep the ones you like and breed again, the way Dawkins bred biomorphs. Then start a new evolution from the pen. To favour a score or a form metric, breed 24 and keep the six best on it.

Method

The sandbox grew out of two studios: the evolutionary search of Generative Techniques (Ahmedabad, 2020) and the attractor-and-stream logic of Eco-Machine (Mumbai, 2020). The Python engine builds each site from open data; this page runs the same engine in your browser, checked to give identical designs and scores. Scores are simple and indicative: good for comparing designs, not for absolute claims.
