Permutation City · a sandbox for designing with nature

What could be here, if the land wrote the rules?

Pick a site. Its ground, water, flooding, green and existing city are read from open data and stacked, as Ian McHarg did in Design with Nature. The stack becomes rules for what can be built where. Inside those rules, designs are written as genes (attractors that pull density, green and water) and bred across generations, scored on flood storage, heat, embodied carbon, daylight and human scale. You play the urban designer: lock what you like, breed by hand, and throw a storm at it.

01 · what is here

The layers

Each cell is one unit of design: a 20 m hex (centre to corner), the unit of the studio this grew from. Hover for every layer at once.

02 · what the land allows

The rules

Stacked, the layers decide each cell: forbidden (water, deep flooding, the water's edge), retained (the existing city outside the design area), constrained (buildable, with height caps, permeable ground and allowed uses), or open. Evolution can explore inside these rules but never break them.

03 · what the land carries

Landform

McHarg's reading: valleys carry water, ridges carry movement. Water's paths are traced over the ground (mapped streams and drains burned in first); where enough land drains through, a valley starts, kept open with a buffer either side. The divides between catchments are ridges; the strong ones become the primary streets, joined to the existing roads, crossing a valley only where they must. On flat land the heights are noise, so valleys come only from mapped water, and with no drains the skeleton comes from the existing roads. Every design's own streets grow inside this skeleton (its street genes: spacing, orientation, pattern, width).

04 · one design

A genome

A design is 51 numbers. Eight attractors, each a position, a reach, a strength and a kind (circles: solid pulls building density, dashed pulls green, dotted pulls water), seven site-wide genes, and four for its streets: their spacing, orientation (along the contours, along the valleys or on a bearing), pattern (grid, loops or branching) and width. Streets run on the edges of cells and take part of each cell they pass. Water claims its share first, then green; the rest is building, rising towards the cores.

05 · generations

Evolution

Designs within 10% of the floor-area brief always beat those outside it. Among them, a design survives if no other is better on every score at once. Parents swap whole attractors, 60/40; a few genes mutate; a tenth of each generation are newcomers; near-copies lose their place, so distinct families survive.

That is NSGA-II, the default. NSGA-III keeps the same breeding but spreads survivors over fixed directions through the five scores instead of keeping the most distinct. MAP-Elites asks a different question: not the best trade-offs, but the best design of every kind. Pick two traits; it keeps the best design in each cell of a grid over them (tall and open, low and dense, and so on), even where those designs lose on the scores.

Any form metric with a better end (chapter 06 lists them) can join the five scores as an objective, up to two. With more than five objectives crowding stops telling designs apart, so "auto" switches to NSGA-III.

Piecemeal growth builds the site in three increments, as Alexander argues cities should grow. Each increment is a run of the size above that evolves only what it adds: by 2025 up to 40% of the floor-area brief on up to 40% of the land, by 2035 70%, by 2050 all of it. What earlier increments built stays as built, and the street network is laid out once, in 2025. Each increment is scored under its year's sea level, storm and heat (IPCC AR6, SSP2-4.5 or SSP5-8.5). An increment that lowers the wholeness of what is there pays for it: a sixth objective, the drop in wholeness. Search is NSGA-III; locks, the pen, the shock and MAP-Elites don't apply. Wholeness is read on every design, so expect minutes.

A link grows the run again from its seed and settings. A downloaded run keeps everything as it is: designs, family tree, the pen, locks.

Family tree

Every generation is a row, the first at the top. Lines run from parents to children; the parent that gave more of the genes where the two differ draws the heavier line. A ring marks a child that mutated (heavier for more genes), a square a newcomer, a diamond a design the run started from (the pen or a typology seed). Dark grey: the designs the run kept; the largest, darkest dots: its front; the outlined dot: the design you opened. At first only the kept designs and their ancestors are drawn; "+n" at the end of a row counts the designs of that generation that left no surviving descendants. Point at a design to trace its ancestors; click it to open it below.

06 · the trade-offs

Explore

Every design the run kept. Dark designs are on the front (on the brief, and no other kept design beats them on every score); grey ones are beaten; hollow ones miss the floor-area brief. Each axis runs from worse to better. Point at a design to find it, click to look at it.

Beside the scores, each design has thirty form metrics: human scale, permeability, nature, harmony and variety, and Alexander's properties of wholeness (levels of scale, strong centres, boundaries, rhythm, positive space, symmetries, contrast, gradients, roughness, the void). The streets and blocks they need are read off each design: blocks are joined building cells, cut where an existing road crosses. They are readouts for judging, not a verdict.

    08 · by hand

    Breeding

    Pick two parents and breed six children; keep the ones you like and breed again, the way Dawkins bred biomorphs. Then start a new evolution from the pen. To favour a score or a form metric, breed 24 and keep the six best on it.

    09 · building scale

    Massing

    The urban design sets plots and envelopes: where a building may stand, how high the rules allow, and how much floor area the design put there. Inside them, buildings are bred the same way, written one of three ways: a grammar of typologies, a field of voxels with a smooth skin, or a small network that turns place into form. Each is scored on winter sun on the ground, shade on public ground on hot afternoons, sky view from the street, how much wind it blocks, how much winter sun it takes from the buildings already there, and its floor area against the target. Only the block you pick is ever built in 3D.

    Pick a design in Families (07) or the pen (08), then press mass a block under its plan.

    Method

    The sandbox grew out of two studios: the evolutionary search of Generative Techniques (Ahmedabad, 2020) and the attractor-and-stream logic of Eco-Machine (Mumbai, 2020). The Python engine builds each site from open data; this page runs the same engine in your browser, checked to give identical designs and scores. Scores are simple and indicative: good for comparing designs, not for absolute claims.
