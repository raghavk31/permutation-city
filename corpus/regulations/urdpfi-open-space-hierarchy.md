## The hierarchy (secondary: URDPFI as the NIUA guide gives it)

| Level | Population served | Area |
|---|---:|---:|
| tot lot (children 0-5) | (in the neighbourhood, within a 10-minute walk) | 125 m² |
| housing area park | 5,000 | 5,000 m² (up to two in a neighbourhood) |
| neighbourhood park and playground | 10,000 | 10,000 m² (playground 5,000-10,000 m²) |
| community park | 100,000 | 50,000 m² plus a 20,000 m² multipurpose ground |
| district park | 500,000 | 250,000 m² plus a 40,000 m² multipurpose ground |
| city park | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 m² |

Other secondary sources disagree on the neighbourhood park's population (15,000 in exam
material citing URDPFI 2015). The guide gives no walking distances except the tot lot's.

## Walking distances (assumed)

Neither source gives catchment distances for the upper levels. The engine uses 100 m
(doorstep), 400 m (housing area park) and 1,000 m (neighbourhood park), the walking
distances of the doorstep, local and neighbourhood play areas in Fields in Trust, Guidance
for Outdoor Sport and Play (England, 2015): **assumed** for Indian sites.

## Correa's hierarchy

Charles Correa, The New Landscape (1985), describes four levels of open space in the
Indian city: the family's own (courtyard or terrace), the doorstep where neighbours meet
and children play, the neighbourhood meeting place, and the principal urban open space (the
maidan). The book gives the structure, not sizes; the sizes above stand in for it
(**assumed**, book not read: decision 7, 26 October 2026). Correa's Belapur housing is often
described with courts of about 8 x 8 m for seven houses, 12 x 12 m for 21 and 20 x 20 m for
36 (secondary, unchecked): the doorstep level, built at block scale (section 5-8 of the built
form plan).
